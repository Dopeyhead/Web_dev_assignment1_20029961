Grammar

Learning grammar is essential to learning any language. However, Japanese is very different to English and may take some time to click. Some learners may be better learning phrases or immersing by watching Japanese TV shows before diving fully into the grammar as it can help the having sentences in mind when learning this. However, it is important to learn the grammar at some point as it will accelerate your language acquisition.

Sentence structure

Japanese sentences are generally structured: Subject → Object → Verb;
which is contrasted with English's structure of Subject → Verb → Object.
Examples:

  • English structure: I eat sushi.
  • Japanese structure: I sushi eat. — Romaji: Watashi wa sushi o taberu — Japanese: 私 は 寿司 を 食べる。

Now we are going look how sentence structure differs in a much more complex sentence:

  • English structure: I am going to ride the fast train to Paris (destination) from Amsterdam (origin) with Alex (co-participant).
  • Japanese structure: I Alex with (co-participant) Amsterdam from (origin) Paris until (destination) train by am going to ride.
    • Romaji: Watashi wa Alex to (co-participant) Amusuterudamu kara (origin) Pari made (destination) densha ni norimasu.
    • Japanese: 私 は Alex と (co-participant) アムステルダム から (origin) パリ まで (destination) 速い電車 に のります。

Grammar particles

Japanese particles are small characters added after words to show their role or relationship within a sentence. They are essential for understanding Japanese sentence structure. Many do not have direct translations in English, but they are marked by colour where there is a good translation. Here are some of the most commonly used particles:

は (wa) – Topic marker

Identifies what the sentence is about. Although written as 'ha', it is pronounced 'wa' when used as a particle.

  • Example: 私は学生です。
  • Hiragana: わたしはがくせいです。
  • Romaji: Watashi wa gakusei desu.
  • English: I am a student.

を (o/wo) – Direct object marker

Identifies what receives the action of a verb. It is written を and usually pronounced 'o'.

  • Example: 寿司を食べます。
  • Hiragana: すしをたべます。
  • Romaji: Sushi o tabemasu.
  • English: I eat sushi.

が (ga) – Subject marker

Identifies the subject of a sentence, often emphasising who or what is performing an action or being described.

  • Example: 猫がいます。
  • Hiragana: ねこがいます。
  • Romaji: Neko ga imasu.
  • English: There is a cat.

に (ni) – Destination, time or location marker

Indicates a destination, a specific time or the location where something or someone exists.

  • Example: 学校に行きます。
  • Hiragana: がっこうにいきます。
  • Romaji: Gakkou ni ikimasu.
  • English: I go to school.

で (de) – Location or means marker

Indicates where an action takes place or the method or tool used to perform it.

  • Example: 図書館で勉強します。
  • Hiragana: としょかんでべんきょうします。
  • Romaji: Toshokan de benkyou shimasu.
  • English: I study at the library.

の (no) – Possession or description marker

Connects two nouns, often showing ownership or describing a relationship between them. It can be thought of in some sentences as the English word 'of' or the possessive "'s".

  • Example: 私の本です。
  • Hiragana: わたしのほんです。
  • Romaji: Watashi no hon desu.
  • English: It is my book.

か (ka) – Question marker

Placed at the end of a sentence to turn it into a question. It can also mean 'or' when presenting alternatives.

  • Example: 学生ですか？
  • Hiragana: がくせいですか？
  • Romaji: Gakusei desu ka?
  • English: Are you a student?

Nuances: Topic, Subject and Pronouns

Copulas
Desu (です) is a copula, which is a word used to link the subject of a sentence with a predicate. While it is not a verb it has been marked as one in the examples above as it is equivalent to the verb 'to be' in certain contexts. But it is worth knowing that it does not possible to translate it directly to English.

Destination, Location and Time
As opposed to English where prepositions for time, destinations and location can be used at any place in the sentence, in Japanese they are only used at the start of a sentence.

For example:
English: I will go to the store at 5pm./At 5pm I will go to the store.
Japanese: 5時に、私は店に行きます。
Hiragana: 5じに、わたしはみせにいきます。
Romaji: Go-ji ni, watashi wa mise ni ikimasu.

Topic vs. Subject
The particles は and が both relate to the subject of a sentence, hence why they have been highlighted as subject in the particle section above, but they have different roles in Japanese. は marks the topic — what the sentence is about — while が marks the subject, often identifying or emphasising who or what performs an action or has a particular quality.

For example:

  • Japanese: 日本では、学校が厳しい規則を設けています。
  • Hiragana: にほんでは、がっこうがきびしいきそくをもうけています。
  • Romaji: Nihon de wa, gakkou ga kibishii kisoku o moukete imasu.
  • English: As for Japan, schools have strict rules.

Here, 音楽（おんがく） is the topic of the sentence, while クラシック（くらしっく） is what is liked the most. Notice how both は and が can appear in the same sentence because they perform different functions.

You'll notice from the example above, the "I" is missing in the Japanese sentence. This is an important difference between Japanese and English is the use of pronouns. Japanese frequently omits words such as 私（わたし (watashi – I) and あなた (anata – you) because the person being discussed is usually understood from the context. Unlike English, it is not required to include a subject and more often than not, personal pronouns are omitted. This is one reason why は is so useful when talking about yourself or someone else. It can be used to establish a topic and then be omitted in subsequent sentences.
